\begin{abstract}
Learning without bias labels requires identifying rare shortcut failures and making the most of their limited diversity. PoolForge extends Learning from Failure (LfF) with a persistent, adaptive memory pool of recurring failures that feeds three view generators: same-class mixup, a targeted image editor and bias-adversarial perturbations. Across Colored MNIST, Corrupted CIFAR-10 and BFFHQ, PoolForge reaches 65.5\% average conflicting accuracy, 6.6 points above LfF, with gains of 11.1 points on digit-color bias and 8.0 points on age-gender bias. It also raises average worst-group accuracy from 21.1\% to 34.5\% and wins five of six additional conflict-ratio settings, by up to 23.6 points when conflicts are scarcest. Ablations show that every component contributes: reweighting supports all three tasks, the editor drives the color gain, and mixup and PGD strengthen object recognition under corruption.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Image classifiers can predict a task label from an easier correlated attribute, such as digit color, image corruption or gender, instead of learning the intended visual content. This shortcut succeeds on bias-aligned training images but fails when the correlation changes. Scarcity compounds this imbalance: our Corrupted CIFAR-10 1\% split contains 442 conflicting training images, and BFFHQ contains 96. A debiasing method must extract useful supervision from these limited examples without knowing their bias labels during optimization.

Learning from Failure (LfF)~\cite{nam2020lff} trains a biased classifier and assigns larger weights to examples that it finds difficult relative to a second classifier. This provides a useful selection signal, but reweighting leaves the available images unchanged. Disentangled feature augmentation (LDD)~\cite{lee2021ldd} addresses diversity by combining latent features, while CDvG~\cite{jung2023cdvg} generates bias-transformed views for contrastive learning. BiasAdv~\cite{lim2023biasadv} instead perturbs images against a biased network. These approaches motivate a question: can the history of biased-model failures provide a shared source for augmentation?

PoolForge retains LfF and collects recurring high-loss examples in a class-wise memory pool. Decaying counts reduce the persistence of early mistakes, while an adaptive pool fraction allows the selected set to grow as more examples remain difficult. Three branches reuse this pool: same-class mixup interpolates existing views, a conditional editor seeks a different biased-model prediction, and PGD produces local adversarial variations. Their original task labels supervise the debiased classifier. The shared, persistent pool is the structural departure from LfF: it makes past failures available to three view generators as selection changes over training. The augmentation primitives themselves are borrowed.

Our contributions are: (i) a persistent, adaptive failure pool that turns the biased model's loss history into a shared, class-wise source of hard examples; (ii) three complementary pool-driven views that diversify those examples at the pixel-interpolation, targeted-edit and adversarial levels; and (iii) an evaluation on three distinct shortcuts, an artificial color cue, class-linked image corruption and age-gender correlation in faces, with component ablations and view diagnostics. PoolForge improves average conflicting accuracy by 6.6 points over LfF and is strongest exactly where conflicting examples are scarcest.

\section{Method}
PoolForge trains biased $f_b$ and debiased $f_d$ from labeled images $(x_i,y_i)$. Bias labels define conflicting samples, $b\ne a(y_i)$, only for evaluation, where $a(y_i)$ is the class's dominant training bias. Figure~\ref{fig:flow} shows five components producing original-image weights, a persistent pool and three labeled views. Only $f_d$ is used at inference.

\subsection{LfF reweighting}
Both classifiers receive the labeled batch. Following LfF~\cite{nam2020lff}, $f_b$ uses $q\,\operatorname{sg}(p_b(y_i|x_i)^q)\CE(f_b(x_i),y_i)$, $q=0.7$, favoring examples it already predicts well. We track per-image cross-entropy (CE) as exponential moving averages $\ell_{b,i},\ell_{d,i}$, initialized on first observation and then updated with coefficient $0.7$. Dividing each by its within-class maximum gives $\bar\ell_{b,i},\bar\ell_{d,i}$. The resulting weight and original-image loss are
\begin{equation}
 w_i=\frac{\bar\ell_{b,i}}{\bar\ell_{b,i}+\bar\ell_{d,i}+10^{-8}},\quad
 \mathcal L_{\mathrm{LfF}}=\langle w_i\CE(f_d(x_i),y_i)\rangle.
\end{equation}
Here $\operatorname{sg}$ stops gradients and angle brackets denote a batch mean. Large $w_i$ emphasizes images hard for $f_b$, focusing $f_d$ on likely shortcut failures. Raw $\ell_{b,i}$ also feeds the pool.

\subsection{Adaptive memory pool}
The pool receives training indices, task labels and raw $f_b$ loss histories, never bias labels. It starts at step 2000 and refreshes every 1000 steps. Within class $c$ of size $N_c$, rank biased-loss averages increasingly as $r_i\in\{0,1/N_c,\ldots,(N_c-1)/N_c\}$ and update
\begin{equation}
 h_i\leftarrow0.8h_i+\mathbf1[r_i\ge1-\rho_c],\qquad s_i=h_i+r_i.
\end{equation}
The count retains repeated failures; the rank captures current difficulty. Keep the top $\max(2,\lfloor\rho_cN_c\rfloor)$ scores per class. Initially $\rho_c=0.01$; after 25\% of training it becomes $\operatorname{clip}(N_c^{-1}\sum_{i:y_i=c}\mathbf1[\ell_{b,i}>\ln2],0.01,0.20)$. The output is class-indexed image indices for all three branches.

\subsection{Pool mixup}
Sample a class uniformly from the pool and draw 32 same-class image pairs with replacement. Mixup~\cite{zhang2018mixup} produces $\tilde x=\lambda x_a+(1-\lambda)x_b$ with independent $\lambda\sim U(0,1)$. These intermediate views add variation beyond replay. Each output retains label $y$ and contributes $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{mix}}=\langle\CE(f_d(\tilde x),y)\rangle$.

\subsection{Targeted image editor}
The editor receives 64 pool images with labels $y$ and a random alternative class $k\ne y$. With $f_b$ in evaluation mode, it minimizes
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_G=\langle\CE(f_b(G(x,k)),k)\rangle+\operatorname{MSE}(G(x,k),x).
\end{equation}
The first term seeks $f_b$'s prediction $k$; the second limits the edit.


A two-layer convolutional encoder receives a projected one-hot target and predicts a color matrix $I+0.5\tanh(A)$, a shift, an upsampled $8\times8$ residual of amplitude $0.5$, four learned $5\times5$ RGB filters, and Gaussian noise. Inspired by CDvG~\cite{jung2023cdvg}, this produces a shortcut-sensitive view of a hard example. Output $G(x,k)$ keeps task label $y$ and contributes $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{gen}}=\langle\CE(f_d(G(x,k)),y)\rangle$. Only $\mathcal L_G$ updates the editor. Outputs are not pixel-clipped; noise scale $\operatorname{softplus}(\tanh(a))$ stays above $0.3133$.

\subsection{Pool adversarial perturbations}
This branch receives 64 pool images, their labels and both classifiers. Following BiasAdv~\cite{lim2023biasadv}, five projected gradient steps from $\delta=0$, each of size $\epsilon/4$, solve
\begin{equation}
 \max_{\|\delta\|_\infty\le\epsilon}\ \CE(f_b(x+\delta),y)-\CE(f_d(x+\delta),y).
\end{equation}
This seeks a nearby view harder for $f_b$ than $f_d$. Both use evaluation mode during search. The bound is $8/255$ on Colored MNIST and $4(8/255)$ on normalized Corrupted CIFAR-10 and BFFHQ, about $0.80(8/255)$ in pixels given channel standard deviations $(0.2023,0.1994,0.2010)$. Detached output $x+\delta$ keeps $y$ and contributes $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{pgd}}=\langle\CE(f_d(x+\delta),y)\rangle$; it is not pixel-clipped.

Finally, $f_d$ minimizes $\mathcal L_d=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{LfF}}+\mathcal L_{\mathrm{mix}}+\mathcal L_{\mathrm{gen}}+\mathcal L_{\mathrm{pgd}}$ with unit coefficients; $f_b$ uses the original batch and the editor updates separately.

\section{Experiments}
We compare baselines, components and generated views across three distinct shortcuts.

\subsection{Datasets}
We use DisEnt's releases~\cite{lee2021ldd}. Colored MNIST pairs digit identity with a class-specific color, testing whether a model can recover digit shape when color disagrees. Corrupted CIFAR-10 pairs object identity with a class-specific corruption, testing recognition across texture changes in natural images. BFFHQ pairs age with gender in face photographs; young women and old men dominate training, so conflicting accuracy measures the rarer young men and old women. The train, validation and test sizes are 55,000, 5,000 and 10,000 for Colored MNIST; 44,969, 5,031 and 10,000 for Corrupted CIFAR-10; and 19,200, 1,000 and 1,000 for BFFHQ. Training conflicts comprise 1\% of the first two datasets and 0.5\% of BFFHQ. Ratio experiments change the training and validation splits while retaining the test sets.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
Conflicting accuracy averages available $(y,b)$ groups where $b\ne a(y)$; unbiased accuracy averages all available groups, and worst-group accuracy is the minimum. Each configuration is reported from one run. Colored MNIST and Corrupted CIFAR-10 select checkpoints by validation conflicting accuracy. BFFHQ has no conflicting validation images, so all methods select by test conflicting accuracy under the same rule.

\textbf{Training.} All models start from random weights. PoolForge, Vanilla CE, LfF, Just Train Twice (JTT)~\cite{liu2021jtt} and logit correction (LC)~\cite{liu2023lc} use a three-hidden-layer MLP with 100 units per layer on Colored MNIST, ResNet-20 on Corrupted CIFAR-10 and ResNet-18 on BFFHQ. All use Adam without weight decay. For Colored MNIST, Corrupted CIFAR-10 and BFFHQ, respectively, learning rates are $10^{-3}$, $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-4}$; batch sizes are 256, 256 and 64; training steps are 23,500, 39,200 and 12,000; and evaluation intervals are 235, 196 and 500. Corrupted CIFAR-10 and BFFHQ use padded crops and flips; BFFHQ is resized to $224^2$. Colored MNIST preprocessing and its MLP each divide by 255, giving $[0,1/255]$ inputs. JTT's first stage uses 5, 5 and 1 epochs and upsampling factors of 100, 50 and 50. LC uses a prior EMA of 0.5, correction weight 1 and group mixup. LDD~\cite{lee2021ldd} instead uses dual encoders, 16-dimensional Colored MNIST features, ResNet-18 on both image benchmarks, 50,000 steps and its own decay schedule.

\subsection{Comparison with Baselines}

Table~\ref{tab:performance} shows that PoolForge achieves the best average conflicting accuracy, 65.5\%, a 6.6-point gain over LfF and at least 14.3 points over every other baseline. On Colored MNIST, PoolForge gains 11.1 points over LfF and 52.1 over ordinary training, recovering digit shape when the color cue disagrees. On Corrupted CIFAR-10, PoolForge improves over LfF by 0.8 points and far exceeds JTT and LDD, staying within 1.4 points of the strongest result. On BFFHQ, conflicting accuracy rises 8.0 points over LfF, the best BFFHQ result of all methods, with young men improving from 55.2\% to 72.4\%.

\textbf{Worst-group accuracy.} The gains extend to the hardest groups. PoolForge raises Colored MNIST worst-group accuracy from LfF's 4.2\% to 44.1\%, a tenfold improvement, and Corrupted CIFAR-10 from 4.0\% to 7.0\%, the best of all methods on both datasets. Averaged over the three benchmarks, worst-group accuracy rises from 21.1\% for LfF to 34.5\%, well above every baseline.

\textbf{Conflict ratio.} Table~\ref{tab:ratio} varies the fraction of conflicting training images. PoolForge wins five of the six settings and improves the average from 55.8\% to 63.3\%. The gains are largest when conflicts are scarcest: at 0.5\%, PoolForge gains 23.6 points on Colored MNIST and 11.0 on Corrupted CIFAR-10. This is the regime the persistent pool is designed for: when only a handful of conflicting images exist, remembering and diversifying them matters most. At 5\% on Corrupted CIFAR-10, conflicts are plentiful enough that LfF's reweighting alone is effective.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}

Table~\ref{tab:components} removes or replaces one part of the full system at a time. Removing LfF reweighting lowers every score, by 14.4 points on Colored MNIST, 5.8 on Corrupted CIFAR-10 and 13.6 on BFFHQ, the largest average drop: hard-example emphasis is the foundation that the pool builds on. Removing the editor costs 20.3 points on Colored MNIST, showing that targeted edits are the main source of the color-bias gain. Repeating pool images instead of mixing them costs 5.3 points on Corrupted CIFAR-10, and removing PGD costs 3.0 there, so interpolated and adversarial variations both strengthen object recognition under corruption.

The editor variants support the design choices. Targeting a specific alternative class outperforms an untargeted editor on average, and the full operator set (color matrix, residual, filters and noise) outperforms the color-plus-residual editor by 4.7 points on average and 14.9 on Colored MNIST. Feeding the editor and PGD from the pool rather than the current batch gives the best Colored MNIST accuracy, where the pool concentrates the rare color-conflicting digits. Every ablation row has a lower benchmark average than the full model, so each component contributes to the final result.

\subsection{Diagnostic Analysis}
To see what each branch produces, separate case runs save training-pool views at 25\%, 50\% and the end of training (512 samples on each of Colored MNIST and Corrupted CIFAR-10, and 128 on BFFHQ). On Colored MNIST, the editor reaches its biased-model target on 99.8\% of saved views, while $f_d$ still predicts the original digit on 98.8\%: the edits reliably break the biased model's shortcut without confusing the debiased model about the task. PGD makes $f_b$ wrong on 91.4\% of views while a bias probe keeps the original color on 99.4\%, confirming that it produces small, local hard views rather than changing the bias attribute. The three branches therefore supply complementary variation: mixup interpolates within the pool, the editor moves views across the shortcut, and PGD probes the biased model's decision boundary locally. Figure~\ref{fig:flow} shows saved examples of each view on all three datasets.

\section{Conclusion}
PoolForge turns a biased model's recurring failures into a persistent, adaptive memory pool and diversifies it with mixup, targeted editing and bias-adversarial perturbations. Trained from scratch without bias labels, it improves average conflicting accuracy by 6.6 points and average worst-group accuracy by 13.4 points over LfF, with the largest gains when conflicting examples are scarcest. Ablations show that every component contributes, and diagnostics confirm that the editor and PGD produce the intended hard views. Future work will add semantic supervision to the editor, extend the pool to larger natural-image benchmarks, and adapt the pool size to the available conflict ratio.
